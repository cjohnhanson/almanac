# Structured Thinking and Communication

Each framework here is a tool, not a religion. The point is to catch
what unstructured thinking misses. Pick the framework that fits the
problem. Combine them when useful. Drop them when they get in the way.

---

## 1. Minto Pyramid / SCQA

Barbara Minto developed this at McKinsey in the 1960s. The core insight:
readers absorb information top-down, so communication should be
structured answer-first, with supporting logic beneath.

### The SCQA Narrative Flow

**Situation** — Establish shared context. What does the audience already
know and agree with? Non-controversial setup. Keep it short.

**Complication** — What changed? What went wrong? What tension exists?
The complication is why you're communicating at all.

**Question** — The complication raises a natural question in the
reader's mind. Sometimes explicit, sometimes implied. It bridges from
"here's the problem" to "here's what to do."

**Answer** — The recommendation or key message. This goes at the top
of the actual document, even though it comes last in the narrative
logic. SCQA is for *developing* the argument; the pyramid is for
*presenting* it.

### The Pyramid Structure

```
           [Answer / Key Message]
          /          |           \
   [Argument 1] [Argument 2] [Argument 3]
    /    \        /    \        /    \
 [Evidence] [Evidence] [Evidence] [Evidence]
```

Each level answers "why?" or "how?" for the level above. Arguments
at the same level must be:
- **Logically ordered** — by time, structure, or degree
- **Mutually supportive** — they collectively prove the point above
- **At the same level of abstraction** — don't mix strategic arguments
  with tactical details at the same tier

### Common Mistakes

- Burying the answer. If the reader wades through three paragraphs of
  context before the recommendation, the pyramid has been inverted.
- Confusing situation with complication. Situation is what everyone
  already knows. Complication is the new tension.
- Making arguments that don't actually support the answer. Each branch
  must bear weight. Decorative branches weaken the structure.

## 2. BLUF — Bottom Line Up Front

From U.S. military communication doctrine (AR 25-50). Messages get
interrupted, radios cut out, people get pulled away. The most important
information comes first because it might be the only thing read.

### Structure

**Bottom Line** — The single most important thing the reader needs to
know. One to two sentences. The decision, finding, or request.

**Background** — Enough context for the bottom line to be intelligible.
Not a history lesson.

**Supporting Details** — Evidence, analysis, data, caveats. Readers who
need it will read it. Readers who trust the bottom line can stop.

**Action Required** — What needs to happen, by whom, by when. If no
action is required, say so explicitly. Ambiguity about next steps is
the most common failure mode in professional communication.

### BLUF vs. Minto

Complementary, not competing. BLUF is a communication *habit* — lead
with the conclusion. Minto is a *structure* for building the logic
beneath that conclusion.

## 3. MECE and Issue Trees

MECE (Mutually Exclusive, Collectively Exhaustive) is a decomposition
principle. Parts should not overlap (mutually exclusive) and should
cover everything (collectively exhaustive). Nothing falls through cracks,
nothing gets double-counted.

### Building Issue Trees

**Start with the core question.** Frame it precisely. "Why is revenue
declining?" is better than "What's wrong with the business?"

**Decompose into MECE branches.** At each node: do these branches
overlap? Is anything missing? Each branch should be testable or
answerable independently.

**Go deep enough to be actionable.** A branch is done when it points to
something you can investigate, measure, or act on. If it's still
abstract ("improve operations"), decompose further.

### Common MECE Frameworks

Well-known decompositions that are MECE by construction:

- **Revenue = Price x Quantity** — Volume further splits into market
  size and market share.
- **Profit = Revenue - Costs** — Costs split into fixed and variable.
- **Internal / External** — Factors within the organization's control
  or outside it.
- **Supply / Demand** — Market problems are supply-side or demand-side.
- **Customer Journey** — Awareness, Consideration, Purchase, Retention,
  Advocacy.
- **Value Chain** — Inbound logistics, Operations, Outbound logistics,
  Marketing/Sales, Service.
- **3 C's** — Company, Customers, Competitors.

### Failure Modes

- **Not mutually exclusive.** If branches overlap, you double-count.
  Test: can an item belong to two branches?
- **Not collectively exhaustive.** If branches have gaps, you miss root
  causes. Test: does anything not fit any branch?
- **Too shallow.** Two-level trees rarely reach actionable items.
- **Forcing MECE where it doesn't apply.** Some problems are genuinely
  tangled. MECE is a tool, not a law of nature.

## 4. Six Thinking Hats

Edward de Bono's framework for parallel thinking. Most group discussions
fail because people argue from different modes simultaneously — one
person is creative while another is critical. The hats force everyone
into the same mode at the same time.

### The Six Hats

**White Hat — Facts and Information.**
What data do we have? What do we need? Neutral, objective. No
interpretation, no opinion.

**Red Hat — Feelings and Intuition.**
Gut reactions, legitimized without requiring justification. "This feels
risky" is valid. The point is surfacing intuitions that get suppressed
in "rational" discussion.

**Black Hat — Caution and Critical Judgment.**
What could go wrong? Risks, weaknesses, flaws. The most natural hat for
many people and the most overused. Valuable, but only as one mode
among six.

**Yellow Hat — Optimism and Benefits.**
Advantages, upside, best case. Counterweight to black hat. Forces
consideration of opportunity, which critical thinkers often skip.

**Green Hat — Creativity and Alternatives.**
Unconventional options, new ideas. Quantity over quality. No criticism
allowed — that's what black hat is for.

**Blue Hat — Process and Meta-thinking.**
What are we trying to accomplish? What hat next? Are we making progress?
Manages the thinking process itself. Typically worn by the facilitator.

### Typical Sequence

For evaluating a proposal:

1. **Blue** — Define the question and process
2. **White** — Gather facts
3. **Red** — Initial gut reactions
4. **Yellow** — Benefits
5. **Black** — Risks
6. **Green** — Improvements and alternatives
7. **Blue** — Summarize and decide next steps

Flexible: for creative problems, start with green. For risk-heavy
decisions, spend more time in black and yellow.

## 5. First Principles Thinking

Reasoning from fundamental truths rather than by analogy. Most thinking
is analogical: "this worked before" or "everyone does it this way."
First principles strips away assumptions to find what's actually true,
then builds up from there.

### The Process

**Step 1: Identify assumptions.**
What do you believe about the problem? Be explicit. "We need a
database" is an assumption. "Users want a mobile app" is an assumption.
Most assumptions are invisible until deliberately surfaced.

**Step 2: Break down with "why?" chains.**
For each assumption: why do we believe this? Evidence or convention?
Keep asking until you hit something demonstrably true or clearly
unexamined.

Example:
- "We need to hire more engineers." Why?
- "We can't ship fast enough." Why?
- "The codebase is complex." Why?
- "We accumulated technical debt." Why?
- "We optimized for speed over quality for two years."

Now the problem is reframed: maybe the answer isn't hiring but paying
down debt.

**Step 3: Separate real constraints from assumed constraints.**
Real: laws of physics, legal requirements, available budget. Assumed:
"we've always done it this way," "the team won't accept that." Challenge
every assumed constraint.

**Step 4: Reconstruct from fundamentals.**
Starting from only what's demonstrably true and actually constrained,
what's the best solution? This often looks nothing like the conventional
approach.

### First Principles vs. Analogy

Analogy is faster and usually good enough for well-understood problems.
First principles is expensive. Reserve it for when the conventional
approach seems wrong, stakes justify the effort, or genuinely novel
solutions are needed.

## 6. Steel Manning

The opposite of straw manning. Instead of weakening an opposing argument,
strengthen it to make it as hard as possible to defeat.

### How to Construct a Steel Man

**Step 1: Understand the opposing position thoroughly.**
Read the strongest advocates, not the weakest defenders. Arguing against
microservices? Read the best case for them, not a regret blog post.

**Step 2: Identify the strongest version.**
Best evidence, most compelling logic, strongest examples. Construct it
as the most capable proponent would — including preemptive responses to
obvious objections.

**Step 3: Apply the endorsement test.**
Would a thoughtful advocate recognize and endorse your characterization?
If they'd say "that's not what I mean," the steel man isn't strong
enough. Refine until they'd say "yes, exactly — maybe better than I'd
put it myself."

**Step 4: Respond to the strongest version.**
If you can defeat the steel man, you've addressed the real argument. If
you can't, you've learned something valuable.

### Why It Matters

It forces genuine engagement instead of performative refutation. It
often reveals the opposing position has merit. It builds credibility.
It prevents the false confidence of defeating a straw man.

## 7. OODA Loop

Colonel John Boyd developed this for fighter pilot decision-making. It
applies broadly to any competitive or fast-changing environment.

### The Four Phases

**Observe** — Gather raw information. What's happening? What signals are
present? Observe broadly before narrowing focus.

**Orient** — The critical phase. Raw observations become understanding,
shaped by:
- Previous experience and mental models
- Cultural context and cognitive biases
- New information integrated with existing understanding
- Analysis and synthesis

Boyd considered orientation most important because it determines how
you interpret observations and what options you consider. Two people
can observe the same facts and orient completely differently.

**Decide** — Select a course of action. This is a hypothesis: "given
our understanding, this action should produce this result."

**Act** — Execute. Then immediately begin observing results, feeding
back into the next loop.

### Why Orient Matters Most

Orientation is where biases live, where mental models help or hinder.
The most common failure: observing new information but orienting with
old models — seeing evidence the plan isn't working but interpreting it
as "we just need more time."

### Speed Through the Loop

Boyd's insight: the fighter who cycles through OODA faster gains a
compounding advantage. Each action creates a situation the slower
opponent hasn't oriented to. In non-combat contexts: iterate quickly.
Small decisions made and tested rapidly beat large decisions made slowly.

## 8. Eisenhower Matrix

"What is important is seldom urgent, and what is urgent is seldom
important." Prioritization along two axes: urgency and importance.

### The Four Quadrants

```
                    URGENT              NOT URGENT
               +-----------------+-----------------+
  IMPORTANT    |  Q1: DO         |  Q2: SCHEDULE   |
               |  Crises         |  Strategy        |
               |  Deadlines      |  Planning        |
               |  Emergencies    |  Prevention      |
               +-----------------+-----------------+
  NOT          |  Q3: DELEGATE   |  Q4: ELIMINATE   |
  IMPORTANT    |  Interruptions  |  Time wasters    |
               |  Some meetings  |  Busywork        |
               |  Some emails    |                  |
               +-----------------+-----------------+
```

**Q1 (Urgent + Important): Do immediately.** Genuine crises and hard
deadlines. The goal isn't to eliminate Q1 but to minimize it through
better Q2 investment.

**Q2 (Not Urgent + Important): Schedule deliberately.** This is where
leverage lives. Strategic planning, skill development, system
improvement, prevention. Q2 reduces future Q1. It's sacrificed most
often because it never screams for attention.

**Q3 (Urgent + Not Important): Delegate or minimize.** Feels pressing
but doesn't matter much. The urgency is often someone else's priority.

**Q4 (Not Urgent + Not Important): Eliminate.** Some Q4 is fine for
rest, but don't confuse it with productive work.

### Why Q2 Is Where Leverage Lives

Most people spend too much time in Q1 and Q3. The result is perpetual
firefighting: Q1 crises could have been prevented by Q2 investment,
but Q2 keeps getting deferred. Breaking this cycle requires protecting
Q2 time as non-negotiable.

## 9. Socratic Method

Disciplined questioning that exposes assumptions and clarifies thinking.
The questioner doesn't need the answer — the value is in forcing
explicit reasoning where vague intuition previously lived.

### Six Types of Socratic Questions

**1. Clarifying Questions**
- "What do you mean by X?"
- "Can you give an example?"
- "How does this relate to what you said earlier?"

**2. Probing Assumptions**
- "What are you assuming here?"
- "Why do you believe that's true?"
- "What if that assumption were wrong?"

**3. Probing Evidence and Reasoning**
- "What evidence supports this?"
- "How do you know?"
- "Is there counter-evidence?"

**4. Exploring Viewpoints**
- "How would someone who disagrees see this?"
- "What's another way to look at it?"
- "Who benefits from this perspective?"

**5. Probing Implications**
- "If this is true, what follows?"
- "What are the consequences?"
- "What would happen if everyone did this?"

**6. Questions About the Question**
- "Why is this question important?"
- "Is this the right question to be asking?"
- "What assumptions does the question contain?"

## 10. Amazon Working Backwards / PR/FAQ

Instead of starting with technology ("what can we build?"), start with
the customer ("what should we build?").

### The Press Release

Write a press release as if the product has launched. This forces
clarity about what it is and why anyone should care.

- **Heading** — Name it in customer terms
- **Subheading** — Who the customer is and what they get. One sentence.
- **Summary** — Product and benefit. If you can't summarize clearly,
  the concept isn't sharp enough.
- **Problem** — The pain point, specifically described.
- **Solution** — How the product solves it. Customer experience, not
  technology.
- **Leader quote** — Why the company built this.
- **Getting started** — Concrete, simple.
- **Customer quote** — What a user got out of it.
- **Call to action** — Where to learn more.

### The FAQ

**External (Customer Questions):** What is it? How much? How does it
work? Why this instead of alternatives? Limitations?

**Internal (Stakeholder Questions):** How big is the opportunity? Cost
to build and operate? Key risks? Dependencies? Success metrics?
Timeline?

### Why It Works

It's surprisingly hard to write a compelling press release for a bad
idea. The exercise exposes weak value propositions, unclear
differentiation, and muddled thinking. If the press release is boring,
the product will probably be boring too. The internal FAQ surfaces
business case problems that otherwise don't get addressed until deep
into development.

## When to Reach for Which Framework

| Situation | Primary Framework | Supporting |
|---|---|---|
| Writing a memo or proposal | Minto Pyramid / SCQA | BLUF |
| Email or Slack update | BLUF | — |
| Decomposing a problem | MECE / Issue Trees | First Principles |
| Brainstorming options | Six Thinking Hats (Green) | — |
| Evaluating a proposal as a group | Six Thinking Hats (full) | Steel Manning |
| Challenging conventional thinking | First Principles | Socratic Method |
| Evaluating opposing arguments | Steel Manning | Socratic Method |
| Fast-changing situation | OODA Loop | Eisenhower Matrix |
| Prioritizing tasks or backlog | Eisenhower Matrix | MECE |
| Unclear requirements | Socratic Method | MECE |
| Proposing a new product/feature | Working Backwards / PR/FAQ | Minto Pyramid |
| High-stakes technical decision | First Principles + Steel Manning | Six Thinking Hats |
| Running a postmortem | OODA Loop (retrospective) | Socratic Method |
| Writing a decision document | Minto Pyramid | Steel Manning, MECE |

These frameworks overlap and combine naturally. A decision document
might use Minto for structure, MECE for decomposing options, steel
manning for presenting alternatives fairly, and Eisenhower for
prioritizing next steps. The frameworks are lenses, not boxes.
